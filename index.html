01 · The case

We have been aiming at the wrong planet for sixty years.

Venus is the nearest planet to Earth — not sometimes, but always. It is the closest thing we have to a twin: 95% of Earth’s diameter, 90% of its surface gravity, formed from the same neighbourhood of the same disc. And for most of the twentieth century it was the obvious destination. The Soviet Union threw more hardware at Venus than at anywhere else in the solar system.

Then, in 1967, Venera 4 and Mariner 5 came back with the number that ended the conversation: the surface is roughly 460 °C under ninety-two atmospheres of carbon dioxide. Venus became the hell planet, and the crewed-spaceflight community turned around and walked toward Mars.

That was a reasonable reaction to a surface. It was the wrong reaction to a planet. Because Venus has something Mars does not: altitude. Fifty kilometres up, above almost all of that crushing atmosphere, the pressure is one bar, the temperature is survivable, the gravity is nine-tenths of Earth’s, and the air overhead stops more radiation than Earth’s own.

Because Venus has an atmosphere of 95% CO₂, a spacecraft filled with normal air would have an effect similar to helium on Venus. A properly designed spacecraft could float on top of the clouds. Elliot Roth — “We’re Going the Wrong Way”

That single physical fact — breathable air is a lifting gas on Venus — is the hinge the entire architecture turns on. It means a habitat there is not a pressure vessel. It is a balloon that happens to be full of the thing you breathe.

What follows is a complete mission plan: a five-phase programme, a specific launch date, a specific vehicle, the materials it is made of, the people aboard it, what it costs, and — in the last section — the three things most likely to kill it. Every trajectory number on this page was computed for it, and the code is in the repository.

03 · The descent

One hundred kilometres above Venus, falling.

Keep scrolling. You are dropping through the atmosphere of Venus, and the instrument panel on the right is reading the real thing — temperature, pressure, how much lift a cubic metre of breathable air would generate, and how much atmosphere is stacked overhead absorbing cosmic rays.

Seventy kilometres. The haze.

This is the top of the cloud world — sub-micron droplets and the ultraviolet-dark streaks that make Venus look mottled from orbit. Still near-vacuum by our standards. Nothing floats here.

Sixty kilometres. Cloud tops.

Minus ten degrees, a fifth of an atmosphere. The winds here are moving at around 100 m/s — the whole atmosphere super-rotates, lapping the solid planet roughly sixty times for every one of its rotations.

Arrival

Fifty-four to fifty kilometres. The band.

Stop here. Pressure between half an atmosphere and just over one. Temperature from room temperature at the top of the band to a hot afternoon in the Gulf at the bottom. Roughly nine-tenths of Earth gravity. And the column of atmosphere overhead at 50 km is 1 200 g/cm² — more than the 1 033 g/cm² you live under at sea level, and fifty-seven times what a Mars colonist would have.

This four-kilometre band is where the entire mission takes place.

Below the band, everything gets worse fast.

At 45 km it is 112 °C. At 30 km, 224 °C and ten atmospheres. The lift available goes up all the way down — the air only gets denser — but nothing you can build survives to use it.

The floor

464 °C. Ninety-two atmospheres.

The longest anything has ever survived down here is 127 minutes (Venera 13, 1982). This is why Venus was written off, and it is the honest cost of the plan: Venus offers an atmosphere and denies a surface. Anyone who needs solid ground should go to Mars.

04 · The physics

Air floats on Venus for the same reason helium floats on Earth.

Venus’ atmosphere is 96.5% carbon dioxide, which has a mean molar mass of about 43.5 g/mol. Breathable air is 29.0 g/mol. At the same temperature and pressure, the lighter gas floats — exactly as helium (4.0 g/mol) floats in Earth’s 29.0 g/mol air.

At 50 km the numbers work out to 0.534 kg of lift per cubic metre of breathable air — around 60% of what helium gives you on Earth. That is not a curiosity. It means the habitat volume is also the lift volume, and it means the inside and the outside of the hull are at the same pressure.

There is no explosive decompression on Venus. A puncture is not a blowout; it is a slow exchange of gases across a hole, and you have hours, not seconds. No other off-world habitat concept has that property. Every Mars, lunar or orbital habitat is a pressure vessel holding one atmosphere against nothing, forever.

06 · The voyage

27 July 2042. Trans-Venus injection.

Four crew depart a 400 km parking orbit on a burn of 3.50 km/s. Departure energy C₃ is 7.08 km²/s² — comfortably inside what a Starship-class stack throws. The window is open for five months; this is the cheapest day inside it.

124 days inward.

Four months, not eight. Venus is close, and the trip is short enough that the crew arrives without the bone and immune deficits that define a Mars transit. They also arrive with more sunlight than they left with — power and thermal both get easier the whole way in.

28 November 2042

Arrival. The stack splits.

Approach speed at the atmospheric interface is 11.29 km/s. The transit habitat aerocaptures into a 300 km orbit carrying two crew; the airship separates four hours earlier and enters direct, carrying the other two.

Thirty days aloft.

Carried by the super-rotating winds, the airship laps the entire planet roughly every five days — about six circumnavigations in a month, each with a fifty-hour night in the middle of it.

The catch

The return goes the long way out.

Leaving Venus on 28 December 2042 buys a 305-day return — but look at the trajectory: it swings out past Earth’s orbit to 1.32 AU before falling back in. The crew arrives travelling at 8.67 km/s relative to Earth, which is an entry speed of 14.06 km/s.

Apollo 10 came home at 11.07 km/s. This is the hardest number in the plan, and it is dealt with honestly in the risk register below.

29 October 2043

Home. 459 days, door to door.

Fifteen months. The same search run against Mars returns 990 days for a short stay and 1 020 for a long one.

10 · The habitat

A hundred and twenty-nine metres of hull, and not one pressure bulkhead.

129 m × 34 m77 500 m³crew of 2float 50–54 km

Why the lift is split two ways

Sealed helium cells carry 66.8 t of the lift because helium is three times better at it. But helium is the one consumable Venus cannot supply, so the rest of the hull — 31 500 m³ of it — is simply the habitat, open to ambient pressure and full of breathable air.

That volume earns its keep twice: it carries 16.8 t of lift and it is where the crew lives. And because it is at ambient pressure, the envelope around it never has to hold a pressure differential. It only has to hold a shape, keep acid out, and keep helium in.

Altitude is the thermostat

At 50 km the outside air is 75 °C. You cannot radiate heat into that, so the vehicle does not try. It climbs toward 54 km through the day side, where it is 38 °C and the sun is strongest, and sinks toward 50 km at night, where the lift is greatest and the batteries are lightest. Buoyancy control and thermal control are the same system.

The envelope, outside in

Target areal density ~200 g/m². Over roughly 11 100 m² of envelope that is about 2.2 t of skin — the rest of the 20.6 t structural allowance goes to tendons, fittings, suspension and the solar crown.

The airship does not fly anywhere. It is carried — the whole atmosphere super-rotates, and at the float altitude that means about 60 m/s of free westward travel. The crew circumnavigates a planet roughly once a working week without spending a watt on propulsion.

The cost of that ride is the night. Venus’ own day is 117 Earth days long, so the only thing setting sunrise is the balloon’s own motion, and half a lap is about fifty hours in the dark. Energy storage sized for fifty hours is the single heaviest subsystem after the ascent vehicle.

Each of the four loops below has already been demonstrated somewhere. MOXIE ran the oxygen reaction on Mars in 2021. NASA’s EVE study ran the same solid-oxide cells on simulated Venus atmosphere to make lift gas. None of them has been run together, at scale, in acid.

12 · The crew — four people, two stations

NASA’s HAVOC study left the transit habitat empty in Venus orbit for the thirty days its two crew were in the atmosphere. This plan does not. Two crew stay with the ride home and keep it alive; two fly the airship. Nobody is ever the only person aboard a vehicle.

13 · The bill

Forty-four billion dollars over twenty years, and launch is barely one per cent of it.

Phase by phase, 2026 dollars

$2.2 bn per year, averaged over the programme

Against published crewed-Mars estimates

The crewed flight needs roughly 450 t in low Earth orbit. At the $200–500/kg that a Starship-class vehicle is projected to reach, that is $90–225 million of launch — around half a per cent of the programme. Launch is not what makes this expensive. The ascent vehicle, the envelope qualification programme and twenty years of standing army are.

Treat every figure here as order-of-magnitude. They are built bottom-up from analogous programmes, not from a costed work-breakdown structure, and historically such estimates run low. Even tripled, the total sits below the cheapest published number for putting people on Mars.

15 · The verdict

Venus is not easier than Mars. It is shorter, closer, warmer and better shielded — and it has no floor.

The case is not that Venus is a paradise. It is that we picked our destination by looking at a surface, and Venus is the one planet where the surface is the worst part of it. Fifty kilometres up, the conditions are better than anywhere else off Earth, by every measure that acts on a human body: pressure, gravity, temperature, radiation, light, and the time it takes to get home.

Against that, two hard problems and one permanent limitation. The ascent vehicle has to launch off a balloon. The return has to survive an Earth entry nobody has flown. And there will never be ground to stand on.

Those are engineering problems and a trade, in that order. The Mars plan’s problems — nine hundred days of galactic cosmic rays at 0.38 g, in a pressure vessel that must not fail once — are physiology problems, and physiology does not yield to funding.

The next window opens on 27 March 2031. It costs $0.9 bn to fly a balloon through it and find out whether the envelope survives a Venus year. That is the whole decision.
