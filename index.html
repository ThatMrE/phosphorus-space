Why Venus

We wrote off a whole planet because we only looked at the floor.

In 1967 our probes came back with the number that ended the conversation: the surface of Venus is 860 °F under ninety-two atmospheres. So we turned around and walked toward Mars, and we have been walking that way ever since.

That was the right reaction to a surface and the wrong reaction to a planet. Venus has something Mars does not — altitude. Climb fifty kilometers and you are above almost all of that crushing air. What is left is the most Earth-like place in the solar system that is not Earth.

A spacecraft filled with normal air would have an effect similar to helium on Venus. A properly designed spacecraft could float on top of the clouds. Elliot Roth — “We’re Going the Wrong Way”

That is the whole trick. The air you breathe is a lifting gas there. Your habitat is not a pressure vessel fighting the vacuum. It is a balloon full of the stuff in your lungs.

Round trip — same math, both planets

Both numbers come out of the same trajectory solver, run over the same years. Venus gets you home in fifteen months. Mars takes almost three years — and that is the short option, the one where you barely land.

Stop here

Fifty-four to fifty kilometers.

Room temperature at the top of the band, a hot afternoon at the bottom. About one atmosphere. Nine-tenths of Earth gravity. This four-kilometer slice is where the entire mission happens.

More cover than you get at sea level. 1,200 g/cm² of air overhead at 50 km, against 1,033 at a beach on Earth — and 21 on the surface of Mars.

Below the band it turns on you fast.

234 °F at 45 km. 435 °F and ten atmospheres at 30. There is more lift down there than anywhere — the air only gets denser — but nothing you can build survives long enough to use it.

And the floor is the floor.

860 °F. Ninety-two atmospheres. The longest anything has ever lasted down here is 127 minutes. Venus gives you an atmosphere and refuses you a surface — that is the trade, and it does not get better.

Two gases, two jobs

Sealed helium cells do most of the lifting, because helium is three times better at it. But helium is the one thing Venus cannot give you, so the rest of the hull is simply the house — open to the outside air pressure and full of the stuff you breathe.

That volume earns its keep twice. It lifts, and it is where you live. And because the pressure inside matches the pressure outside, the skin never has to hold anything back. It only has to keep its shape, keep acid out, and keep helium in.

Altitude is the thermostat

At 50 km it is 167 °F outside. You cannot dump heat into that, so the ship does not try — it climbs toward 54 km through the day where it is 100 °F, and sinks at night where the lift is best. Flying it and cooling it are the same job.

How it gets made

That last step is why the program is shaped the way it is. Thirty-four tons of breathable air is more than the ship can carry, so it has to be made on site — and making it takes years. The 2034 flight is not a rehearsal that happens to go first. It is the start of something that has to finish before 2042 is even possible.

How it floats

Air floats on Venus for the same reason helium floats here.

Venus air is mostly carbon dioxide and weighs about 43.5 g/mol. What you breathe weighs 29. The lighter gas rises — same physics, different planet.

So there is no explosive decompression on Venus. A puncture is not a blowout, it is a slow trade of gas through a hole, and you have hours instead of seconds. No other habitat we have ever designed off Earth has that property.

Life up there

Thirty days at nine-tenths of a gravity, inside a cloud you never see out of.

You do not fly the ship anywhere. The whole sky is moving, and you move with it — a full lap of the planet about once a working week, for free.

What that costs you is the night, and how long the night lasts is your choice. Float low for more lift and a longer darkness; float high and the reverse. Altitude sets the length of your day.

Against published crewed-Mars numbers

Getting people to Venus takes about 450 tons in low Earth orbit. At the prices a Starship-class vehicle is aiming for, that is $90–225 million of launch — half a percent of the whole thing. Launch is not what makes this expensive. The ascent vehicle and twenty years of payroll are.

Treat every figure as order-of-magnitude. Estimates like these historically run low. Even tripled, this sits under the cheapest published number for putting people on Mars.

So

Venus is not easier than Mars. It is shorter, closer, warmer, better shielded — and it has no floor.

The case was never that Venus is gentle. It is that we picked where to go by looking at a surface, and Venus is the one planet where the surface is the worst part of it. Fifty kilometers up, everything that acts on a human body — pressure, gravity, heat, radiation, light, time away from home — is better than anywhere else we could go.

Against that: two hard engineering problems and one thing we can never fix. The ride home has to launch off a balloon. The return hits Earth’s atmosphere faster than anyone ever has. And there will never be ground to stand on.

Those first two are money and time. Mars’s problems — three years of cosmic rays at a third of a gravity, in a pressure vessel that has to never once fail — are biology, and biology does not care how well funded you are.

The next window opens March 27, 2031. Flying one balloon through it costs about $900 million and tells us whether the skin survives a Venus year. That is the whole decision.
